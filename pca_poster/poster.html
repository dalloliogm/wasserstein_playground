A little history. Karl Pearson (1901) posed it geometrically as the “line of closest fit” to points in space; Harold Hotelling (1933) named the “principal components” and computed them with an iterative power method on a desk calculator. Both did precisely what this poster does — sums of squares and products, then a characteristic equation — by hand.
Recipe. centre → covariance → eigen-decomposition → sort by λ → project.
Data: A(0,1) B(1,2) C(3,3) D(4,2) — numbers chosen so the eigenvalues (11, 1) are whole.

3The characteristic cubic

det(S − λI) = 0 ⟹ λ³ − (tr S)λ² + (Σ minors)λ − det S = 0

tr S = 10 + 4 + 10 = 24
minors: (10·4 − (−2)²) + (10·10 − (−1)²) + (4·10 − 2²) = 36 + 99 + 36 = 171
det S = 10(4·10−2·2) − (−2)((−2)·10−2·(−1)) + (−1)((−2)·2−4·(−1))
       = 10·36 + 2·(−18) − 1·0 = 324
λ³ − 24λ² + 171λ − 324 = 0
Coefficients are “sum of the roots”, “sum of products of pairs” and “product of the roots” (Vieta) — so they double as checks at the end.
Recipe (any size). centre → S = XᵀX → det(S−λI)=0 → solve for λ → (S−λI)v=0 → sort by λ → project.
Numbers chosen so that λ = 12, 9, 3 and the eigenvectors are small whole-number vectors.

5Eigenvectors

For each λ, S−λI is singular: its rows are all ⟂ to the eigenvector, so the cross product of two rows is the eigenvector.
λ = 12: S−12I: row1=(−2,−2,−1), row3=(−1,2,−2)
row₁ × row₃ = (6, −3, −6) ∝ (2, −1, −2), |v| = 3
→ PC1 = (2, −1, −2)/3
λ = 9: S−9I: row1=(1,−2,−1), row2=(−2,−5,2)

If the roots aren’t whole numbers? A symmetric 3×3 always has three real roots: use Cardano’s trigonometric formula, or Newton’s λ ← λ − p(λ)/p′(λ). From degree 5 up no formula exists (Abel–Ruffini), so Hotelling iterated: multiply a vector by S repeatedly — it swings onto PC1 — subtract it, repeat.
